It is **outside the pre-registered measures**: no decoy control, no blind review, and the lead knew the literature beforehand. ## The Irish passage
- **Source.** The Life of Saint Fínán (Fínán Cam of Cenn Etig, Kinnitty), Early Modern Irish. Edited by R. A. S. Macalister, *The Life of Saint Finan* (Halle a. S., 1899); CELT G201017, dated 17th century by CELT. Celtic layer units G201017:#1:38 and, for a second horse episode, #1:16.
- **What happens (#1:38).** One day Fínán is crossing a mountain. His horse's leg breaks (*do bhris cos an eich*). He blesses it (*do bheannaig*), so that its bones join together again (*gur leanadar a cnámha da chéile*). The horse is put back in its own place under the chariot (*fán carbad*), and the leg is sounder than it was before.
- **The second episode (#1:16).** On the same day as another miracle, one of the horses of Fínán's chariot breaks its leg. God sends a fine horse out of the lake to take its place for three years, and at the end of the three years it goes back into the lake. This is a water-horse motif, not a healing.

## What it shows and what it does not
- **Shared:** the narrative sequence of the healing type. A journey is interrupted by the injury of a horse's leg, a divine or holy healer intervenes, and the bones are joined. The Irish keeps the "X to X" joining, though not as a polyptoton: *a cnámha da chéile* is reciprocal, "its bones to one another".
- **Not shared:** cognate wording. Irish *cnáim* "bone" is not cognate with Old High German *bein* or Sanskrit *ásthi*. Of the bone words only *ásthi* continues PIE \*h₃est- (cf. Greek ὀστέον); Irish and Old High German have other words. *cos* "leg" and *each* "horse" are not used in the Germanic or Vedic texts. (*each* = *áśva-* is cognate, but the Vedic hymn does not name a horse.) This is a thematic parallel (narrative type and figure), not a formula in the sense of Stages A to C.
- **The chariot detail.** The chariot appears in the Vedic hymn (the healed patient is told to run like a good chariot; the Ṛbhu join "as the parts of a chariot") and in Fínán (the healed horse goes back under the chariot). It is suggestive, but horses and chariots are everyday in Irish hagiography (the second episode has one too), so it does not count as evidence on its own.
- **Inheritance or medieval spread.** The Christian form of the type (Christ, St Olav, St Brigit or Bride heals a horse's leg, "bone to bone") circulated across medieval and early modern Europe. West records Scandinavian, English, Gaelic, Latvian and Russian versions. Schmitt (1967) discusses whether the Christian variants come from the pagan type or the reverse. A 17th-century Irish saint's life can as easily reflect the Christian charm tradition as a pre-Christian Irish one. The secure Irish witness of the *figure* remains Míach's spell in *Cath Maige Tuired*.

## Literature checked (held texts, plus one web search)
- **West 2007, ch. 8** (the charms; nn. 65 to 67). It gives Merseburg II, the later Scandinavian, British and Gaelic versions (one from Benbecula with Bride), Míach's *ault fri halt di ocus féith fri féth* (*Cath Maige Tuired* l. 135 Gray), and AV 4.12 including the chariot verse, after Kuhn. Fínán is not mentioned.
- **Watkins 1995, chs. 56 and 57** (pp. 523 to 533). It gives Merseburg II with AV 4.12.3 to 5 (Kuhn), the AV 12.5 sequence, and Míach (p. 532 f.). Fínán is not mentioned.
- **Matasović 1996, pp. 130 to 132.** It discusses Merseburg and the Atharvavedic healing formulas as a historiola (deity, Ṛbhu or Christ, performs the healing), cites a Christianized English/Gaelic variant, and agrees with Schmitt that a genetic explanation is most probable. Fínán is not mentioned.
- **Schmitt 1967, §§ on Zauberdichtung** (pp. 286 to 289, 301 to 307). It discusses the pagan and Christian variants and their priority. Fínán is not mentioned.
- **Web search (2026-10-24).** It found no publication connecting Fínán's horse miracle with the Merseburg type. It did find Norwegian horse charms where Christ or St Olav replaces Wodan (the saint as healer).
- **Not held:** Kuhn 1864; Ebermann 1903 (*Blut- und Wundsegen*); Carmichael, *Carmina Gadelica*; Plummer's *Vitae Sanctorum Hiberniae* and *Bethada Náem nÉrenn* (their introductions list folklore motifs in the saints' lives); Durante 1976. Any of these may already note the Fínán episode. Plummer is the most likely place.
- **Novelty status:** *not found* in the held literature. It is a candidate Irish hagiographic witness of a known type, and its value as evidence for inheritance is low (see the previous section).
