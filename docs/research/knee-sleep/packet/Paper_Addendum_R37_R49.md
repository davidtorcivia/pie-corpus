# Paper addendum: rounds 37–49
## A consolidated supplement for rounds 37–49
### 28 October 2026 · Read with the unchanged paper, Knees as the Support of Sleep

**Scope and result.** This supplement updates the analytical apparatus of the paper without silently rewriting its earlier text or inflating the number of positive witnesses. It summarizes the original reports in rounds 37–49, which preserve fuller transcriptions, individual source locators and reading scopes. No independently established early KNEE[SUPPORT]–SLEEP branch was added by these rounds. Their main contributions are better-controlled alternatives, textual restorations and local transmission relations.

## A. Knee placement without sleep: the controls become more exact

Fuscagni's edition of CTH 430.2, KBo 17.61 obverse 20–22, establishes a child-placement construction including `DUMU-an`, `genuwaš=šaš` and `teḫḫi`. Initial clitics are restored, and the relationship between the queen, speaker and garment-pronoun antecedent must remain qualified. The child's support on knees is represented in the edition; sleep is not. The tablet's reverse body-part equivalence is a distinct ritual action, not a second supported-child episode. [R37; earlier R36]

The Hittite Ullikummi narrative makes the distinction clearer. Colon 100 of the TITUS composite places the child on Kumarbi's knees; colon 172 removes him from there; colon 176 puts him on Enlil's knees; colon 190 uses Upelluri's shoulder. The partly restored initial occurrence and the substantially preserved later transfer should not be assigned identical documentary status. Witness A is itself a composite incorporating KUB 33.96, KUB 33.93 and other pieces; E is a separate copy, KBo 26.60. Two publication numbers within A are not two manuscripts. [R38–R39]

This intra-Hittite recurrence supplies evidence that knee-placement diction was conventional or readily repeatable within the literary tradition. It does not establish that KNEE and SLEEP were conventionally paired. The surviving Hurrian version confirms a cross-language mythological relationship, but the specifically compared naming/curse segment is later than the Hittite knee-placement lines. A Hurrian equivalent of those knee words has not been demonstrated in this investigation. Lack of that verification is not evidence that the Hurrian version lacked them. [R39]

The Hebrew birth-on-knees passages in Genesis 30:3 and 50:23 extend the typological comparison, not the Indo-European cognate set. In particular, an English sexual euphemism using “sleep with” must not be counted as an ancient lexical sleep predicate. Literal, social and figurative interpretations of the birth expressions require separate argument. [R38]

## B. The Homeric scene that changes support

Iliad 22.500–504 places Astyanax's feeding at Hector's knees next to the onset of sleep. The sleeper is then in a bed in his nurse's arms. This is genuine contextual association of knee support, caregiving and inherited sleep vocabulary in a short passage, but the support at the moment of sleep is not the knees. It is therefore weaker and structurally different evidence than AP 7.170 for the exact supported-sleeper relationship. [R41]

Odyssey 19.399–409, by contrast, gives an explicit infant-on-knees naming scene involving Eurycleia and Autolycus. Iliad 9.485–495 gives knee-supported feeding by Phoenix. These are close comparanda for Hittite child-care and naming, rather than sleep positives. Their relevance lies in the historical availability of the support scene, not an invented rate at which knees were preferred to other supports. [R41]

The Old Norse knee-setting episodes in Haraldz saga, §§21 and 40 in the specified edition, connect knee placement with naming, fosterage and status. The semantic conventionality of `knésetja` in these scenes makes their social dimension concrete. Two episodes of a medieval saga do not become independent early Germanic formula traditions; dates of narrated events do not date the words. [R42]

## C. Dream language close to Nerik, but without knees

CTH 386.1, KUB 36.90 obverse 3′–7′, supplies a closely related Nerik invocation involving awakening from pleasant dreams and a bird-like message. Its preserved target clause has no knee expression. KUB 36.89 itself has bird language at reverse 52′–53′, near the knee–dream clause at 56′–59′. The proximity is a useful exact lead, not a completed collation of the continuous sequence. [R43]

A separate Telipinu passage, KUB 33.10 obverse II 7, preserves a form from the *ses-* sleep family while the following waking question is restored. It helps distinguish a preserved sleeping predicate from a supplied narrative continuation, and a different inherited sleep root from the *swep-family material relevant to Nerik. The restored question cannot be used as an independently preserved archaic formula. [R44]

These comparisons establish actual resources of the relevant local literary and ritual environment. They neither prove that Nerik's pairing was newly composed nor require the inheritance hypothesis to possess an otherwise unavailable sentence structure. They improve the description of the alternative that needs to be evaluated.

## D. Bilingual help is not a written sign

In the annals of Ḫattušili, the Akkadian KBo 10.1 obverse 13–14 preserves a lap expression and hand-taking. The parallel Hittite KBo 10.2 obverse I 27–30 is damaged at the place where a knee expression has been supplied in scholarly discussion. The Hittite knee word is not securely legible in the transcription examined here. [R45]

The bilingual relation makes the restoration intelligible. It does not turn the supplied word into a written cognate token. Nor does either version contain the required sleep relation. The manuscript's classification must also be kept distinct from the historical reign and the possible age of the composition.

Appu and Hedammu illustrate a second type of error. In Appu, an adult's bed-sleep and a later baby's placement on knees are different events and participants. In Hedammu, knee trembling and the monster's sleep occur in separate passages with different roles. A same-text search can retrieve both families while failing to retrieve the target relationship. [R40, R45]

CTH 390 adds a third control. Its repeated knee/hand combinations concern binding, conjuring and unbinding a child. The authoritative apparatus favors HAND over an older CLOTHES reading, but the duplicate's hand designation is largely restored. Neither crawling nor supported sleeping may be imported as an explicitly described posture. [R46, R48]

## E. A positive local phrase-history result: three related prayers

The strongest recent result at the level of conventional wording is not a knee–sleep formula. It is the recurring night/bed/pleasant-dream complaint in CTH 372, 373 and 374. KUB 30.10 reverse 18, CTH 373 §12′ colon 89, preserves the bed, pleasant adjective and dream noun, with uncertain portions of the negation and verb. The parallel in CTH 372 §18′ colon 150 restores much of the adjective; CTH 374 §30″ colon 67 restores most of the dream noun and some other material. [R49]

The semantic core is that no pleasant dream takes hold of the speaker at night in bed. This does not necessarily deny sleep altogether. The compositions and their parallels demonstrate a local transmission relationship; their joined pieces and copies are not independent branches. The three versions make visible both repeatable phraseology and the different documentary strengths of its individual words.

The same prayers contain requests for divine speech in a dream and mortality rhetoric elsewhere. Metcalf's discussion of a Sumerian Utu hymn supplies a specific comparison for the life/death material and a historical model of translation/adaptation. The follow-up inspected a publisher-authorized preview, not the original Sumerian cuneiform. It does not show that the negative pleasant-dream sentence itself had the same source. [R49 and standalone Utu follow-up]

This result is important for the target problem in two ways. It demonstrates how a real local phraseological relation can be supported by aligned expressions and critical variants. It also identifies dream diction available without knees. The finding is a control with positive historical content, not a substitute for proving or disproving the separate PIE pairing.

## F. Revised balance of the argument

The core affirmative inference remains: the Hittite Nerik and Greek AP 7.170 expressions bring cognate words into a corresponding supported-sleeper relation, warranting a defeasible inheritance proposal. The new controls strengthen the empirical content of independent-combination and textual-adaptation explanations, but do not establish that either produced these two target clauses.

The responsible update is consequently asymmetrical. The evidence for local knee-support conventions and local sleep/dream conventions has become more explicit; the number of secure independent early target branches has not increased. No fixed PIE sentence, common inflection, phrase-specific metre or calibrated likelihood follows. Additional controls should now be prioritized for their ability to discriminate the histories, not merely their ability to add another row to the archive.
