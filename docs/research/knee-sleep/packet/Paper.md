## A source-critical case for proposed Indo-European lexical–semantic inheritance

## Abstract

This paper evaluates a proposed inherited association between the knee as bodily support and sleep or dreaming. Its strongest early comparison joins the Hittite invocation of the Storm-god of Nerik, KUB 36.89 reverse 56′–59′, with the Greek funerary epigram Anthologia Palatina 7.170. Both place a sleeper, or a person described as sleeping, on knees and combine inherited knee vocabulary with material from the Indo-European *swep- sleep family. The correspondence therefore concerns a lexical relationship organized around comparable participant roles, not merely two unrelated etymologies. Its grammatical realization nevertheless differs: the Hittite sleep derivative participates in a predicate with ēšta and a dream-content expression, whereas the Greek *swep- reflex is the noun ὕπνος, governed by ἔχει alongside a different sleep participle. Greek sleep is funerary; Nerik belongs to a divine recall context whose precise theological implications remain disputed.

Later Persian, Celtic, Germanic and Latin material widens the attested semantic range but requires source-by-source treatment. Explicit translations, manuscript copies and recognizable narrative families cannot be counted as independent inherited reflexes. Conversely, later attestation, lexical replacement and imperfect surface alignment do not automatically disqualify evidence for a flexible inherited association. Controls from Hittite child-care constructions, Indo-Iranian sleep diction, Greek funerary language and non-knee bodily supports identify plausible mechanisms of local composition and textual remodeling without constituting a disproof of inheritance.

The cumulative result is a philologically grounded proposal: KNEE[SUPPORT]–SLEEP/DREAM is a defensible candidate for inherited lexical–semantic phraseology. The record does not yet discriminate persuasively between inheritance of the pairing itself and separate retention of knee-support usage and sleep vocabulary, subsequently combined in individual traditions. No fixed sentence, shared phrase-specific meter or calibrated probability is recovered. The accompanying registers distinguish attestation, local transmission, interpretation and reconstruction, and preserve the exact outstanding source work.

**Keywords:** Indo-European phraseology; Hittite; Greek epigram; knee; sleep; dream; formula; textual transmission; comparative reconstruction.

## 1. The claim to be evaluated

The proposed object of inheritance is a relationship: KNEE used as the support or resting place of a person in SLEEP or DREAM. It is not an obligatory sentence of the form “X puts a head on Y’s knee.” A child resting on a mother’s knees, an adult supported on another person’s lap, and a seated person resting on their own knees can instantiate the target relation. The word “lap” in a translation is not by itself sufficient: the underlying lexical expression must be identified before it is counted as knee evidence.

This definition neither licenses arbitrary thematic resemblance nor requires invariant syntax. It distinguishes the target from kneeling in prayer, knees weakening during sleep or death, a knee injury mentioned within a dream, and unrelated knee and sleep references separated in a narrative. The supported participant must be the sleeper or the person represented as sleeping. That participant-role condition follows from the claim under investigation; it is not an additional demand for a special literary motif.

Three propositions must remain separate. First, the relevant clauses exist in transmitted texts. Second, some later clauses belong to identifiable local traditions or translations. Third, the cross-language pairing may descend from an inherited lexical–semantic convention. A secure first proposition does not automatically establish the third; a secure local borrowing relationship does not automatically refute it elsewhere. The evidence registers mark these levels independently.

The strongest defensible formulation is affirmative but qualified: the early Hittite–Greek alignment warrants proposing an inherited KNEE[SUPPORT]–SLEEP/DREAM association. The proposal is more specific than a universal claim that people sleep or use body parts for support. It combines cognate lexical material with an organized relation between a supported sleeper and a supporting knee. What remains undecided is whether that relation was already a conventional pairing at the relevant proto-language stage, rather than a combination repeatedly available from inherited lexical resources.

“Formula” is used here in this flexible lexical–semantic sense. It does not mean a recoverable invariant phrase with fixed inflection, a demonstrable metrical unit, or a single transmitted story. A fuller reconstruction would require fuller evidence, but absence of every optional diagnostic is not a reason to forbid the narrower proposal. Likewise, historical reconstruction does not require deductive exclusion of all conceivable alternatives. The issue is the relative explanatory force of the actual evidence, not logical uniqueness.

## 2. Evidence architecture and historical method

### 2.1 What the comparison is allowed to establish

The inquiry tracks six independent dimensions: lexical identity or cognacy; semantic relation; grammatical realization; textual and manuscript dating; demonstrable source dependence; and the scope of direct reading. A witness may contribute strongly on one dimension and weakly on another. For example, a modern edition may securely attest the printed wording while leaving the date of the earliest recoverable wording open. A translated medieval text can securely establish a knee–sleep scene while supplying little independent evidence for PIE inheritance.

Shared archaic syntax, phrase-associated morphology, a metrical preference, lexical choice in genuinely comparable contexts and a distinctive surrounding sequence can all increase historical weight. None is a universal admission test. Their absence does not turn a positive supported-sleeper clause into a negative. Equally, the presence of a related feature somewhere else in a text cannot be imported into the target clause: a metre for BOSOM–RECEIVE is not automatically metre for KNEE–SLEEP, and an earlier marital sleep event is not the sleep of a newborn subsequently placed on knees.

Evidence of inherited vocabulary is necessary to the particular lexical argument but insufficient to identify inherited co-selection. That distinction is not a rejection of the proposal. It specifies the difference between the comparatively firm lexical foundation and the historical relationship being proposed. Later witnesses with non-cognate sleep terms may illuminate preservation or replacement of the semantic pattern, but they cannot be silently scored as additional reflexes of the same sleep root.

### 2.2 Witnesses, occurrences and branches

Four manuscripts of one Irish prose story remain four witnesses to one story. Three Norse copies of a biblical translation remain witnesses within that translation tradition. A Persian narrative and its Arabic translation are not independent confirmations of a shared Indo-European–Semitic inheritance. An ancient expression quoted by three modern scholars remains one ancient attestation. These distinctions prevent documentary abundance from being converted into genealogical independence.

The converse is also important. Dependence can itself be historically informative. A translated knee gesture shows how inherited vocabulary can be used in borrowed phrasing. A recognizably transmitted sleep charm whose root changes demonstrates lexical remodeling without loss of the larger function. Such cases are controls on inference, not deductions that all similar wording must be borrowed.

The paper therefore uses “independent” sparingly. Hittite and Greek are separate language traditions with no demonstrated direct textual derivation of these particular target clauses in the present record. That is not equivalent to proving absence of all contact. Nor does a general possibility of Greek–Anatolian contact establish a donor, direction or date for this association. A possible route is a research question; a demonstrated sequence of textual correspondences is evidence for a specific relation.

### 2.3 Dating and preservation

Composition date, narrated date, manuscript-copy date, edition date and acquisition date are separate fields. An annal under the year 585 does not date the wording of its surviving medieval witness to 585. A twentieth-century Sām-nāma edition does not certify a medieval form of its verse. A manuscript containing a martyrology does not date an attached prose preface to the life of the saint. A catalogue’s late-Hittite classification does not prove that every expression in the tablet was newly composed then.

These distinctions permit late witnesses to contribute without making them older than their documentation. They also preserve the possibility that late or composite works contain older phraseology. The task is to show which features are carried by the actual evidence and which require historical inference.

## 3. The Hittite anchor: the Storm-god of Nerik

### 3.1 The attested comparison

The principal Hittite locus is KUB 36.89 reverse 56′–59′, conventionally discussed as part of CTH 671.1.A. The governing research record preserves the written knee form GI-NU-WA and the sleep participle šu-up-pa-ri-ia-an-za. CHD Š, printed p. 176, quotes the target under pleasant sleep/dreams; printed p. 617 treats šuppariya- and explicitly analyzes the Nerik passage with a dream object. Those two pages were freshly inspected for this synthesis. This is a renewed dictionary-source check, not a newly acquired handcopy or tablet collation. [S001–S004; E01]

A short normalized sequence from the dictionary’s presentation is:

> ᵈTešimi=wa=kan āššiyanti genuwa‹š› šanizziuš tešḫuš šuppariyanza ēšta.

The angle-bracketed final element on genuwa‹š› is editorial in that presentation. It must not be reported as a written AŠ sign. The other CHD passage prints genuwa without that supplement. A cautious rendering under the dictionary’s second-person analysis is that the god was sleeping or dreaming pleasant dreams in the knee/lap of beloved(?) Tešimi. The following summons to arise belongs to the recall context. This normalized quotation identifies the comparison while preserving the distinction between written text and grammatical repair. [S001, pp. 176,617]

The lexical case is significant. The relevant verb is šuppariya-, not the distinct Hittite šeš-. The project’s etymological control associates šuppariya- with the *swep- family, also reflected in Greek ὕπνος. The nominal and verbal derivational histories are not identical; their relationship is lexical-family cognacy, not a shared phrase-specific suffix. The dream-content expression tešḫuš is not itself being claimed as a cognate of Greek ὕπνος. [S032–S033; E01,E40]

### 3.2 Case, person and the supporter

Three uncertainties affect the surface reconstruction without erasing the supported-sleeper relation. The bare knee form has been discussed with allative, bare dative-locative and supplemented dative-locative analyses. The adjective and the ownership expression admit interpretive difficulty. Finally, ēšta is compatible with competing person and BE/SIT analyses. A responsible synthesis cannot select one interpretation for the positive semantic comparison and then borrow the strongest archaic implication from a different, incompatible parse.

The supporter also must not be simplified to “the mother.” Tešimi is associated in the scholarly discussion with the god as beloved; elsewhere the Nerik ritual invokes a mother and chthonic deities. Hoffner’s commentary provides a specific divine-relationship control, but does not establish that the Tešimi of the knee clause is identical to the mother named elsewhere on the tablet. The Greek passage is maternal; the Hittite passage need not be. What aligns securely enough for comparison is the role of the supported sleeper, not an invariant family relationship. [S018–S020; E06]

This matters for the historical hypothesis. A flexible lexical–semantic association can survive a change in the identity of the supporting person. A proposed specifically maternal sleep formula would be a stronger and different claim, requiring evidence not supplied merely by reading the two passages together.

### 3.3 The conditional archaic diagnostic

The absence of reflexive -za has received positive weight in the research record. Its force is conditional: on a second-person predicative-BE reading of ēšta, the omission can be treated as an archaic syntactic feature. A third-person reading or a SIT construction does not yield the same diagnostic. The final -nza of the participle is not an occurrence of the reflexive clitic. [S001; E05]

This is potentially useful local evidence, not a shared Hittite–Greek construction. Hoffner’s discussion of a separate Illuyanka form allows both survival of older usage and deliberate archaism as explanations for an apparent old feature. That other text does not decide the Nerik clause. It illustrates why one grammatical signal cannot be used to date an entire composite ritual or to certify PIE phraseology. The possible archaism should neither be ignored nor promoted beyond the interpretation on which it depends. [S020, pp. 139–140; E05]

### 3.4 The ritual context

Steitler’s selected discussion treats KUB 36.89 as a late composite ritual and prayer for recalling the angry, absent Storm-god. Earlier sections invoke chthonic figures and the opening of the dark earth. A deep-cave expression and the sea-bank/underworld material supply context, but separated references do not demonstrate that the sleep of reverse 56′–59′ is death. The immediate awakening/recall frame is a firmer description than an unqualified equation of sleep with an underworld death. [S019; E06]

The catalogue has identified duplicates for other parts of this tablet: Bo 7950 for reverse 18–22, and KBo 71.236 / Bo 2024/40 for obverse 37 onward. These are exact opportunities to check surrounding restorations. Neither is currently an inspected duplicate of the knee–sleep locus. Multiple tablets therefore cannot be counted as multiple independent copies of that clause, much less multiple Indo-European reflexes. [S002; E07]

The late composite setting does not establish late invention of the phrase. It supports a more differentiated model: an older expression could be embedded in a later arrangement of cultic and theological material. Conversely, the antiquity of the language family does not make the local pairing demonstrably prehistoric. The argument depends on the comparative relation, not on a simple equation between tablet date and phrase date.

## 4. The Greek anchor: AP 7.170

### 4.1 Text and semantic relation

The closing words of AP 7.170 are unambiguous at the level relevant to the comparison:

> ἐπὶ γούνων ματρὸς κοιμαθεὶς τὸν βαθὺν ὕπνον ἔχει.

The child is represented as asleep on his mother’s knees and as having deep sleep. The knee term is γούνων, governed by ἐπί; ματρός specifies the owner. The inherited sleep-family material is ὕπνον. The participle κοιμαθείς is not itself the *swep- reflex. This is a single participant-role configuration, not a chance occurrence of “knee” and “sleep” in different parts of a poem. The whole six-line epigram was freshly checked in the collaborative edition for this synthesis. [S006; E02]

The poem describes three-year-old Archianax, drawn into a well by his reflection, and his mother recovering him while seeking signs of life. The closing sleep is funerary. It should be coded as DEATH-AS-SLEEP, not silently converted into an ordinary restorative nap or a narrated dream. The exact physiological moment of death is not resolved by the wording or by divergent modern translations. The secure point is the poetic representation of the child on the mother’s knees in the final sleep. [S006–S007]

There is no explicit head noun in the closing phrase. That is not a defect relative to the stated target. The child is supported on knees; head-placement is an optional feature in other witnesses, not a gate to this one. Conversely, the poem should not be described as explicitly saying that a particular head was laid upon the knees.

### 4.2 Attribution, genre and transmission

The collaborative edition lists both Callimachus and Posidippus in the attribution record and marks a Hellenistic and funerary setting. The paper does not resolve the authorship dispute. The transmitted copies and anthological placements are witnesses to one poem. Their existence is not a multiplication of independent Greek attestations. The current packet does not contain a new full critical apparatus or manuscript collation. [S006; S084, R17]

AP 7.173 provides a useful semantic control: a death is expressed as long sleep beside an oak, without knees. It shows that the funerary sleep metaphor is available independently of knee support. It does not show how often a poet selected knees in comparable scenes, and does not prove that AP 7.170’s pairing was created locally. Its proper role is to prevent “deep sleep” alone from being treated as a distinctive knee-associated archaism. [S007; E03]

The later Nonnian caretaking passage retained in the governing record adds within-Greek continuity of the broader pattern. It does not add another Indo-European branch. Later evidence is relevant to persistence and literary reuse, but its genealogical force depends on dating and relation to earlier material, not merely on the presence of another Greek occurrence. [S008; E04]

### 4.3 What actually matches Nerik

The common ground is inherited knee vocabulary, inherited *swep-family sleep material, and a participant represented as sleeping while supported on knees. The differences are equally concrete: Hittite uses a sleep derivative with ēšta and a dream-content expression; Greek uses a different sleep participle and HAVE governing a sleep noun. Hittite addresses a deity in a recall ritual; Greek commemorates a child’s death. Hittite support is associated with Tešimi; Greek support is explicitly maternal. “Sweet/pleasant” and “deep” are not the same inherited adjective.

Those differences limit reconstruction of surface phrasing. They do not annul the more abstract correspondence that the proposal actually concerns. The paper consequently reconstructs no sentence, assigns no common case ending, and does not posit a shared verse cadence. The plausible inherited unit is a lexical–semantic relationship, not an artificial amalgam of the two extant grammars. [E01–E05]

## 5. The affirmative cumulative case

The positive argument begins with the co-organization of lexemes and roles. If the comparison consisted only of Hittite and Greek having inherited words for knees and sleep, there would be little phraseological claim to evaluate. Here those materials are brought together in a specific relational scene: the knee is a support for the sleeper. This gives the proposal substantive content and makes it a legitimate comparative hypothesis.

Its antiquity and distribution also matter, although not as a numerical probability. The strongest anchor is not a collection of modern translations or many copies of one medieval story. It crosses Hittite and Greek traditions. The later record shows that knee-supported sleep can be expressed in different social relations and narrative settings, including self-support. Such variation is compatible with a flexible inherited association, even though the later evidence does not independently prove its age.

The conditional Hittite archaism can add local positive weight. Its contribution should be stated as conditional support for the possibility of older phraseological material, not as a decisive shared innovation. Likewise, a later narrative retaining support roles despite lexical substitution could illuminate a route by which a flexible association persists. It would not convert non-cognate sleep wording into an additional *swep reflex.

The best opposing account is not that the resemblance is worthless. It is that inherited knee-support usage and inherited sleep diction remained independently available and were combined in local scenes without a conventional paired ancestor. The Hittite child-placement controls and Greek funerary-sleep controls give that account real empirical content. They establish that ingredients were available separately. They do not establish when or how the target pairing was first combined.

At present, the record supports the reconstruction as a positive proposal but has not supplied a persuasive preference between these two precisely stated histories. A generic appeal to universality is too weak to refute a cognate role-aligned comparison. Equally, listing many unweighted occurrences is too weak to settle the historical depth of co-selection. The remaining question is about the history of the relationship, not the reality of the attested clauses.

This result should not be paraphrased as “nothing has been found.” The secure achievements include the two-language lexical-role comparison, a qualified ancient Hittite constructional signal, several actual later knee–sleep scenes, direct manuscript continuities, and specific demonstrated transmission relationships. They support a narrower conclusion than proof of a PIE convention, but a more substantial one than a collection of accidental English translations.

## 6. Persian and Indo-Iranian evidence

### 6.1 The ʿAṭṭār episodes

The governing record identifies two important Persian targets in Nicholson’s volume I: the Rābiʿa biography at p. 60 and Yūsuf at p. 316, lines 13–15. The scenes involve self-support, with the head placed on the sleeper’s own knees before sleep or dream. Self-support belongs within the specified claim. Excluding it would silently change the research question from knee-supported sleep to sleep supported only by another person. [S047; E19–E20]

The Yūsuf apparatus matters. Nicholson prints bi-xuft, while witness C is reported as na-xuft, “did not sleep.” The ensuing dream/awakening material remains. A synthesis must retain both polarity and subsequent dream language. It may classify the scene as knee-supported dream or drowsiness under the negative reading; it may not flatten both readings into an unqualified affirmative sleep sentence. Nor does the absence of a reported variant at another point establish that every manuscript was collated there. [S047; E20]

The earliest exact manuscript wording is still a source gap. Leiden Or 281 biography spans are not clause locators. The narrower estimates for initial leaves remain estimates. The British Library Or 16775 catalogue date does not supply the target text. The Kazan study offers a meaningful manuscript chronology and codicological discussion, but its Yūsuf target has not been read; Rābiʿa occurs before the surviving beginning. These are concrete limits on dating the printed scene, not grounds for deleting its textual attestation. [S051–S053]

The linguistic comparison also carries an intra-Iranian qualification: the project record preserves a loan-history issue for Persian zānu. A cognate-shaped item in Persian should not be used to claim an independently inherited Iranian branch form without carrying that issue forward. The packet therefore treats the Persian scenes as substantial semantic and textual witnesses while leaving their precise linguistic and manuscript histories explicit.

### 6.2 Arabic translation as a transmission control

Naskban 36531 is an Arabic translation of ʿAṭṭār, despite Persian-language metadata. At the Yūsuf target, the record preserves:

> وما نام بل وضع راسه على ركبتيه فنعس ورأى في المنام

The sequence contains negated sleep, head-placement on two knees, drowsiness and a dream. Its agreement with a Persian negative reading is a transmission comparison, not identification of a precise exemplar. The Rābiʿa target similarly preserves knee-supported drowsiness and dream. The 995AH date belongs to the copy; the editor’s approximate dating of the translation is different. [S054; E21–E22]

The Arabic evidence is useful precisely because it is dependent. It shows that the support-and-dream configuration could be transmitted across languages and that negated sleep need not eliminate a dream event. It is not an independent Semitic witness to a PIE formula. The qualified identification with an edition’s A witness rests on multiple metadata correspondences, but lacks an explicit siglum-to-shelfmark crosswalk and remains qualified.

Earlier Arabic greatest-name/mouse-test stories establish a narrative family behind the episode following the dream. In the bounded texts checked, the knee-dream prelude was not established. That is a limited finding about inspected texts. The modern restored Ibn ʿAsākir volume cannot be promoted into an independent complete early manuscript of the entire scene. [S055]

### 6.3 Sām-nāma

The Ben-Shahi volume II, printed p. 313, contains an explicit positive:

> برو خفته بد نره دیوی سیاه / نهاده سر خود بزانوی ماه

The sleeping figure’s head rests on a knee. Direct checking of the printed page controls the electronic wording, including the following anomalous repetition. This is source control of the known occurrence, not discovery of a second independent instance. The titlepage and closing dates locate the edition in 1319–1320 solar years; they do not establish the medieval antiquity of this exact verse. Rūyānī’s edition and apparatus remain a separate unresolved source task. [S049–S050; E23]

### 6.4 The early Iranian audits

The Khotanese screen acquired all 1,648 advertised TITUS selector pages and recorded 201,948 word-link tokens. Nineteen units were accepted as knee contexts: thirteen homage units, including three fragments; three medical; one calendar; and two Zambasta contexts, involving body hair and knee-depth flower-rain. None of those nineteen was established as knee-supported sleep. This is a bounded result, not a claim that the Khotanese language lacks the association. [S040–S041; E45]

The residual lexical work is important to the scope. From 104 initially unresolved occurrences, the later audit records 93 as non-knee under the stated analyses, including one conditional analysis, and eleven unresolved occurrences representing seven surface forms. A further 279 family-only forms were not converted into negatives. The distinction between supported exclusion, conditional exclusion and unresolved segmentation is preserved in the corpus audit. No preference percentage follows from these search sets.

Dresden’s Jātakastava stanzas 68–70 were directly checked in the relevant printed page. They narrate sleep, attack and blinding but express no knee or lap support. The lexical disputes concern a striking verb and sharpness, not an omitted support term. Chinese, Tibetan-derived and Uigur narrative-family controls likewise cannot import support from another episode into the target. Leumann’s N77.6 and N90.29 remain outside the exact acquired corpus coverage and unread in their original contexts. [S042–S044; E43]

A separate Khotanese–Sanskrit Saṃghāṭa alignment combines a knee-ground gesture with a specific demerit question and answer. It provides a demonstrable local translation relationship. The inherited pedigree of a knee word does not make the sentence containing it independently inherited. This is a methodological control on genealogy, not evidence that Nerik or the Greek poem borrowed from the Buddhist material. [S045; E44]

The Avestan audit likewise retained genuine uncertainties. A dictionary-expanded screen used 1,111 broad anchors. Its eighty-six residual anchors divided into sixty-seven direct other-lexical decisions, eight qualified proposals and eleven unresolved occurrences. Secure and proposed knee contexts were not all collapsed into one certainty class. In particular, the dictionary’s analysis of Vīdēvdād 18.11 zānu.drājah as chin extension prevents it being counted as a knee-sleep positive. These bounded lexical decisions do not amount to a whole-Avestan absence claim. [S046; E46]

## 7. Indic comparisons and methodological controls

### 7.1 The Vedic knee inventory

The prior work matched two RV editions at 10,552 stanza IDs and identified eleven knee loci: 1.37.10, 1.72.5, 3.39.5, 3.59.3, 6.1.6, 6.32.3, 7.2.4, 7.82.4, 7.95.4, 8.92.3 and 10.15.6. Fifty-five stanzas in their bounded contexts were read. The missing short jñu/jñv retrieval forms were a selector gap, not discovery of an unknown knee lexeme: the repository had already recorded jñubadh at 6.1.6. [S035; E38–E39]

RV 1.72.5 is particularly instructive. A kneeling group, a friend’s eye-closing and protection can be parsed in more than one way; the Sāyaṇa-based account allocates roles differently from a modern kneeling-and-watch analysis. Whole interpretations must be evaluated as wholes. One cannot combine the knees from one parse, the sleeper from another and the agent of a third. The checked complete analyses did not establish a sleeper supported on knees, but this does not categorically exclude sleep from every interpretation of the verse. [E38]

The Vedic jñu-bādh and Old Saxon knio-beda comparison is a genuine possible morphological question, qualified by dispute over the second element. Its contexts concern prayer or kneeling, not sleeping on knees. It therefore cannot be presented as recovered knee–sleep phrase morphology. This is an example of preserving a potentially valuable comparison while keeping it attached to the construction it actually concerns. [S037–S038; E39]

### 7.2 Sleep-root variation and metre

Ronzitti’s lullaby study and Pinault’s 2026 analysis document two inherited sleep families within the RV 7.55 material. The local transmission comparison reports replacement of sas- imperatives by svap- forms in Atharvavedic reuse. The relationship is stronger than a merely thematic resemblance because the corresponding content and ordered participants are identifiable. Nevertheless, the present project read the published comparison, not the original Atharvavedic manuscript apparatus. [S033–S034; E40]

The historical lesson is specific: a transmitted sleep formula can preserve its function and participants while replacing a lexical root. This makes lexical replacement a concrete mechanism, not a hypothetical escape clause invented for the knee hypothesis. It does not show that every semantic parallel with non-cognate sleep language descends from the same inherited pairing. Each candidate still needs its own source analysis.

RV 7.55.8 also offers a positive example of metrical conditioning in support vocabulary. Material-support compounds in -śaya- are compared with talpaśīvarī-, whose formation fits the cadence discussed by Pinault. These compounds involve LIE and camp-bed/litter/bed locations, not knees. They demonstrate what a genuine context-specific metrical argument can look like; they do not supply the missing metre of a PIE knee–sleep expression. [E41]

### 7.3 Maternal earth and non-knee supports

RV 10.18.10 invokes Mother Earth, but the actual upastha expression is governed by Nirṛti: the deceased is to be protected from Nirṛti’s lap/bosom. The following stanza compares the earth’s covering to a mother’s garment. Neither contains a knee-sleep expression. English poetic paraphrases cannot change the grammatical owner of the Sanskrit support word. [S036; E42]

The Sāvitrī episode in Mahābhārata 3.281 provides a substantial bodily-support sequence: the head rests in a lap, is released, and is restored in a narrative of death and return that also uses sleep language. The inspected support words are utsaṅga and aṅka, not jānu. Bhavabhūti’s Uttararāmacarita supplies an arm-pillow sleep and withdrawal scene. These passages illuminate the wider support/sleep/release complex but are not lexical knee cognates in the relevant loci. [S077–S078; E36–E37]

Their value is not exhausted by exclusion from a narrow cognate count. They show that a similar support/sleep/release sequence can be instantiated with different supports and that narrative care may motivate withdrawal. These selected parallels do not by themselves establish transmission between the two compositions. But equating an arm with a knee, or a modern stage direction with a manuscript variant, would destroy exactly the source distinctions that give these comparisons evidential value.

## 8. Germanic, biblical and Latin narrative traditions

### 8.1 Stjórn and Samson

Selected official photographs of AM 228 fol., 22va, AM 227 fol., 86rb and AM 617 4to, 84rb–84va were directly inspected in the earlier record. They preserve the head-on-knee placement, explicit falling asleep, haircut and withdrawal sequence. Working readings include head/knee and sleep clauses, with cautious abbreviation expansions. These are actual manuscript gains, not statements based only on a modern translation. The worn AM335 images were not securely read and must not be coded as absence. [S056; E24]

The scene belongs to a biblical Samson–Delilah narrative family. Judges 16:19 supplies an external textual tradition for knee-associated sleep. That relationship materially affects the independence of the Norse occurrence. It does not erase the Norse attestation or prove the precise donor of each additional bodily action. The research distinguishes the inherited lexical materials used by the translator from the narrative content being translated. [S063; mounted R15]

The source-history distinction between Stjórn III and IV is essential. Samson is in III. IV is the short Joshua adaptation associated with Comestor. General statements about verbal dependence in IV cannot be moved into the Samson locus. Likewise, a source pointer to p. 677 concerning the guiding boy does not constitute access to the relevant apparatus on pp. 670–672. The Grønlie chapter target remains unread. [S058–S060]

A real Latin–Norse relationship was established for the adjacent allegory: five ordered Samson/Christ correspondences and a closing Ruth-related conclusion align with the PL 175 tradition attributed through Richard’s Liber exceptionum rather than simply accepted under Hugh’s old label. This is strong evidence for that neighboring interpretive material. It does not automatically identify the source of the earlier knee placement, sleep or withdrawal. [S062; E26]

### 8.2 Sörla and the limits of directionality

The sharper GKS 1005 fol., 37rb image corroborates knee-supported sleep and a woman withdrawing from beneath the sleeper’s head. This makes the Sörla episode a genuine text-level comparison. It does not establish whether Stjórn influenced Sörla, whether a saga supplied a biblical embellishment, or whether some actions reflect another shared narrative resource. The source arrow remains open. [S057; E25]

The specificity of the sequence can contribute to a local historical argument. It is not necessary to demand that every cross-branch witness preserve that sequence before considering inherited lexical phraseology. Equally, the sequence should not be projected back to PIE merely because some of its words have inherited etymologies.

### 8.3 Guntram and Hélinand

Paul the Deacon’s Guntram story supplies head/knee sleep and a distinctive sequence involving an animal’s departure from the sleeper’s mouth, a crossing, return, waking, dream report and treasure. Sigebert’s checked edition gives support in the attendant’s gremium and dormiret. The ordered narrative and source annotation positively support membership in the Paul tradition. They do not prove an immediate exemplar without intermediaries. [S064–S065; E27–E28]

An animal departing from the sleeper’s mouth is not the same action as a supporter withdrawing from beneath a head. Superficial “departure during sleep” must not substitute for alignment of actual roles and events. Nor does the date assigned to the annal date the extant wording.

Hélinand XIII.III remains a precise source gap. Later rounds identified manuscript witnesses and distinguished an edition of chapter headings and marginalia from an edition of the continuous narrative. Lonati’s surviving soldier anecdote and promised later royal Guntram story must remain distinct. A nineteenth-century retelling’s name Gontran is not accepted as a medieval manuscript reading. This line of work has improved source control but has not added a new directly read knee–sleep clause. [S066–S067; E29]

## 9. Celtic evidence and corrections

### 9.1 The Óengus preface

The prose preface transmitted with Félire Óengusso contains an explicit invitation:

> tabair do chenn for mo glún & cotail

The boy is told to place his head on the saint’s knee and sleep; he subsequently describes sleeping with his head on the knee. The ensuing miraculous knowledge of his lesson and revelation of the saint’s identity provide narrative context. This is a real Celtic supported-sleeper scene, not a doubtful English rendering of an unspecified support. [S068; E30]

Its chronological and lexical qualifications are equally important. The prose preface is not automatically as old as the metrical martyrology or the saint it describes. Cotlud is not automatically evidence of the *swep-family root. The source-critical comparison must preserve the text-level positive without backdating it or manufacturing a cognate sleep term.

The later handoff reports selected target readings in Laud 610, Rawlinson B512, NLI G10 and KBR 5100. These establish continuity in one story. They do not turn four copies into four independent Celtic retentions. The seven-copy inventory discussed by Volmering is a manuscript-history framework, not evidence that every witness was directly collated by this project. UCD A7 and the faded RIA target remain unresolved. [S069–S070; E31]

### 9.2 Druimenach, Abbán and Welsh

The governing correction to the Druimenach episode is decisive: the sleep support is an elbow on the ground. A later sword-and-knee action occurs after waking. The corrected apparatus forms are dorat a uillinn and do chotail. This episode must not survive in the positive knee–sleep list through accidental reuse of an older note. [S071; E32]

The Abbán comparison preserves an important sleep-and-careful-release sequence in Irish and Latin. Its support words are ucht and sinus; genua belongs to a subsequent prayer after waking. The relation between recensions is a local transmission problem, not independent branch evidence. The target manuscript clause has not been read simply because the beginning of the saint’s Life is catalogued at a folio. [S072; E33]

The Welsh Yr Hun Felys example was also corrected: elinedd in the specified Ellis source means elbows, not knees. It is a rejected knee positive. These corrections strengthen the reliability of the retained corpus by showing that favorable-looking examples were not preserved at the expense of the underlying text. [E35]

### 9.3 Scottish Gaelic and the bounded census

The Scottish Islay item has a nineteenth-century collection and print history: collection in 1860 and publication in 1872, not an established 1775 knee clause. The association of a historical volume label with Adv.MS.50.1.12 remains a candidate volume identification rather than an exact autograph location. Gunderloch’s qualification about orally collected ballads in that volume must accompany the proposed link. [S073]

The Carmichael Watson and Glasgow autograph targets remain exact but unread tasks. A catalogued scored-through item is not its full text. The preservation paper’s account of a Webrecorder capture does not establish that a publicly released replay containing the target leaves is available. The announced November 2 migration was still in the future at the October 28 cutoff; it was not treated as permission to probe the restricted replacement. [S074–S076]

The Irish retrieval census is useful for organizing evidence: thirty-six bodies, 369 candidates, 104 adjudicated scenes and fifty-five sleep/drowsiness scenes were reported. However, incidental omissions demonstrated retrieval gaps, and unknown supports dominate the classified sleep set. It is not a complete semantic denominator for lexical preference. A failed frequency estimate does not invalidate the Óengus positive; it simply cannot be used as an additional statistical argument. [E52]

## 10. The alternative contexts: what they do and do not show

The most informative controls are actual texts, not abstract claims that bodily support is universal. Hittite Appu places a newborn on knees; the birth ritual KBo 17.61 places a child on someone’s knees; the edited Ullikummi passage places a child on Kumarbi’s knees. These establish available child-support constructions in different Hittite settings. The birth-ritual pronouns and parts of the other edited clauses remain qualified. None of the inspected placement events supplies a sleep predicate. [S021,S023–S025; E08–E10]

The same birth ritual’s reverse contains body-part matching between a goat and a human. Its knee terminology is real, but its function differs from support. The CTH 403.2 ginuwaš=taš expression has another grammatical and semantic setting. These are useful safeguards against treating every knee morphology as direct evidence for Nerik’s bare form. [E11–E12]

The maternal-funerary controls are also real but distinct. KUB 30.28+ has a damaged mother reference and a hand-taking/escort expression. The word DAY is not preserved in the quoted line, even though a translation supplies it. Another tablet, KUB 39.49, actually writes the mother-day phrase. CHD documents that mortality idiom in several Hittite genres. That is evidence for a local mother/death semantic resource, not a written knee/sleep expression or proof that the supporting Tešimi is an underworld mother. [S001,S026–S028; E13–E15]

The Met’s goddess-and-child pendant and Pausanias’s description of Night carrying Sleep and Death add representational context. The first has no written lexemes and does not demonstrate that the child is sleeping. The second specifies hand/arm support, not knees. Neither can be promoted into an extra lexical occurrence. They nevertheless show why an interpreter must distinguish broad caregiving and sleep/death imagery from the narrower paired expression. [S029–S031; E16–E18]

The existence of these controls makes local composition more concrete, but does not settle it. They show that knee support can occur without sleep and sleep/death can occur without knees. They do not show that the target pairing was nonconventional, that it lacked an inherited history, or that local composition is more probable by a measurable amount. Their evidential role is to delimit what is distinctive in the positive comparison and to prevent ancillary themes from being overcounted.

## 11. Corpus findings, false negatives and lexical preference

The project’s corpus work has produced useful finite inventories, but the units differ. Pages, tokens, orthographic forms, candidate hits, paragraphs, scenes and manuscript witnesses are not interchangeable. In a proper source register each number has a named denominator. Combining them into one “number of occurrences across branches” would create a quantity without a coherent historical interpretation.

The Persian sleep-first audit, for example, reported213 eligible units:202 discrete events, nine habitual units and two aggregates. Six specified support and207 did not. “Support unspecified” is not “not supported by knees.” In the head-support stratum, two own-knee scenes and four pillow descriptions, one habitual, cannot be converted into a general preference rate without a justified sampling and comparability model. The alternative-lemma screen found no secure matched ordinary human alternatives of the required kind; silence in that screen is not proof of categorical lexical preference. [S048; E51]

The Greek phrase audit concerned35 pinned XML files and30,136 verse elements, with35 unique contexts from overlapping KOLP and DEXATO sets. Its four metrical endings establish a BOSOM–RECEIVE comparison already known in the earlier scholarship. KOLPOS is not the knee lexeme, and those clauses do not express sleep. Thus the audit supplies a real formulaic control while refusing a false transfer of metrical evidence to the target. [E49]

The recovered Taittirīya Saṃhitā file corrected a missing acquisition in the declared search scope. Forty-one candidates in twenty-eight units were read in that bounded pass. It was not a new whole forty-four-file census. The Paippalāda residuals likewise remain itemized, with modern apparatus needed for opaque forms. Recording these limitations is preferable to turning unknowns into negative controls. [S039; S083]

No probability of PIE inheritance is calculated in this paper. Selected positives are not independent Bernoulli trials; corpus availability and transmission are not random samples; multiple copies are not multiple branch retentions. The absence of a valid frequency denominator prevents a particular quantitative argument, not the historical proposal itself. Qualitative comparative reconstruction remains legitimate when its claims match its evidence.

## 12. Objections and replies

### 12.1 “People naturally sleep on knees, so inheritance is unnecessary”

Physical plausibility is a genuine alternative explanation, but it is not a refutation. The question is whether the specific lexical relation has a history beyond repeated practical experience. Cognate vocabulary organized in comparable roles gives an affirmative basis for inquiry. The actual support controls make local composition testable; a general appeal to human anatomy does not date the pairing.

### 12.2 “The syntax is different, so there is no formula”

That objection would defeat an invariant sentence reconstruction, which is not the claim. A lexical–semantic association can be expressed through different derivations and grammatical slots. The difference limits the resolution of reconstruction and prevents claims of shared inflection. It does not remove the relation that is being compared.

### 12.3 “One text is funerary and the other is not”

The subtype difference is material. It prevents an unqualified reconstruction of ordinary restorative sleep, dreaming, or death as the sole ancestral sense. It does not make the Greek sleep vocabulary disappear. A flexible association may be extended metaphorically, but the direction and date of any such extension remain to be demonstrated.

### 12.4 “The Hittite grammar is too uncertain to use”

The uncertainties affect case, person, supporter analysis and the conditional archaic diagnostic. They do not justify pretending that the published knee and sleep language is absent. The appropriate response is graded inference: retain the lexical-role comparison, weaken constructions dependent on one disputed parse, and prioritize the exact handcopy and edition.

### 12.5 “Later evidence is either worthless or decisive”

Both extremes are mistaken. Later texts can preserve, remodel or translate older patterns. They matter for continuity and semantic range. They become decisive for a particular transmission relation only when actual wording, order, dating and source context support it. Late attestation alone neither proves antiquity nor excludes it.

### 12.6 “A borrowed Norse or Buddhist scene disproves the whole PIE claim”

A demonstrated local translation explains that occurrence’s dependence. It does not supply a donor for the independent Hittite–Greek comparison. It warns against a specific inference—cognate words in a sentence do not guarantee inherited phrasing—without establishing universal borrowing of the target.

### 12.7 “No special metre or frequency result means no reconstruction is possible”

Special metre and comparable lexical preference would be useful additional support. They are not mandatory requirements. The current record must not claim them where they have not been found, but their absence is not an automatic veto on a narrower lexical–semantic proposal.

### 12.8 “Calling the proposal warranted already proves it”

A warranted proposal is a serious explanation supported by positive correspondences. It is not a statement that alternatives have been decisively outranked. The difference is substantive: this paper defends the association as a reconstruction candidate while identifying the historical distinction that the present evidence does not resolve.

## 13. Exact next work and sensitivity of the thesis

The highest-value immediate source tasks remain the original KUB 36.89 handcopy and Haas 1970 pp. 156–157, followed by a continuous check of the recall sequence and relevant duplicate loci. These could sharpen noun case, grammatical person, supporter interpretation and the extent of the archaic signal. None should be represented as already completed through a later quotation. [S003–S004]

The full Massetti 2022 and 2023 chapters remain important because related abstracts, presentations and later citations cannot substitute for their arguments or apparatus. The existing record contains substantial reading of different Massetti works; that does not close these precise gaps. Similarly, Grønlie218–220 and Astås’s target apparatus remain distinct from metadata, review quotations and errata. [S005,S009–S015,S059–S060]

The Persian priority is exact manuscript leaves, not another general catalogue date. Leiden and British Library target clauses, together with Rūyānī’s Sām-nāma apparatus, could materially improve the chronology and polarity history. The Celtic priorities are unresolved autograph identity and remaining preface witnesses, without counting every additional copy as an independent tradition. The residual Iranian and Paippalāda words should remain unknown until better lexical or apparatus evidence is acquired. [S039,S043,S050–S054,S069,S074–S076]

The thesis is sensitive in identifiable ways. A demonstrated alternative reading eliminating knee support or sleep in an anchor would reduce the core comparison. A secure earlier parallel, or phrase-associated archaic material genuinely shared across traditions, would strengthen the inheritance case. A demonstrated donor relationship between the anchor clauses would reduce their independence. A valid comparison of lexical choice could add evidence, while a missing or unsuitable denominator adds none. These are possible evidential developments, not mandatory hurdles imposed after the fact.

## 14. Conclusion

The core positive finding is a real correspondence: Hittite Nerik and Greek AP 7.170 combine inherited knee and *swep-family sleep material in comparable supported-sleeper roles. The record is therefore sufficient to justify proposing an inherited KNEE[SUPPORT]–SLEEP/DREAM lexical–semantic association. That proposal does not depend on inventing an explicit head, a placement verb, a mother in both texts, a fixed sentence, or a shared metre.

The later evidence contributes unevenly but substantively. Persian and Irish scenes enlarge the attested configurations; Norse manuscripts and Latin narrative sequences establish local continuities; dependent translations expose mechanisms of lexical and narrative transmission; and Hittite, Greek and Indic controls separate the target from neighboring support and sleep imagery. Corrections to Welsh, Druimenach, Jātakastava and several source attributions remove false positives without erasing the surviving case.

The historical limit is precise. The present dossier has not shown a persuasive preference between inheritance of a conventional paired association and separate inheritance of its lexical resources followed by local combination. This is not a claim that such a distinction can never be resolved. It is the current state of this source record. The paper consequently offers a defensible positive reconstruction proposal, a transparent account of its limits, and an exact recovery and research apparatus rather than a claim of proof that the evidence does not support.
